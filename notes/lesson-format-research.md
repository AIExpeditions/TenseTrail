# Keep the frame, change what fills it

TenseTrail does not need to scrap its single lesson format. A predictable eight-step shell is defensible for self-study adults, and two of its steps are among the best-supported ideas in language learning: the explicit Rule step and the spaced Remember step. What the research does not support is using identical *content and guidance* in that shell for every grammar point at every level. Three findings drive this. First, the help that beginners need actively gets in the way of advanced learners. This "expertise reversal effect" is well established in learning science, though it has rarely been tested on grammar websites specifically. Second, grammar points differ in kind. Some are easy rules that are hard to use automatically (third-person *-s*). Some have an easy form but a hard concept (present perfect, modals). Some are really vocabulary (*interested in*, phrasal verbs). Each responds to a different kind of explanation and practice. Third, the current Practise step tests only one structure with mostly controlled items. Learners can get all nine right without ever having to *choose* the structure or use it to say something of their own. That is the weakest link between the lesson and real-life English. The fix is to keep the eight steps as a recognisable frame, vary the Rule visual and the practice mix by concept type, thin out guidance as level rises, and let learners who already know a point test out of it. Adapting by "learning style" (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic) is the one kind of personalisation the evidence clearly rules out.

A note on how to read this report: claims are tagged throughout as **strong evidence** (meta-analyses or many replications), **moderate evidence** (several studies, some conflict), **expert opinion** (respected authors' positions, not head-to-head tested) or **inference** (reasoning from evidence to TenseTrail, not directly tested). No study anywhere has compared a fixed lesson template with a varied one on a grammar website. Every claim about "the template itself" is therefore inference.

## Five of the eight steps already rest on solid ground

The single best-supported choice in the format is the explicit **Rule** step. Every major meta-analysis finds that explicit instruction beats implicit instruction on average for adult learners. Explicit instruction here means telling learners the rule or pointing their attention to the form. Spada and Tomita's synthesis of 41 studies found **larger effects for explicit instruction for both simple and complex features**. It helped "spontaneous use" as well as controlled test performance ([Spada & Tomita 2010, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ883419)). Goo and colleagues' update of 34 samples reached the same conclusion ([Goo et al. 2015](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)). This is **strong evidence**. The live debate in the field is about *how* and *when* to explain a rule, not *whether* to. One caveat matters for TenseTrail. Critics note that many of these studies measured learning with controlled tests that favour explicit teaching ([Columbia SALT re-analysis](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434)). A site that judges success only by multiple choice and gap-fill scores will overestimate learning in the same way.

The **Remember** step is the other pillar. Dunlosky and colleagues' landmark review rated practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice as the only two "high utility" study techniques. They rated highlighting and rereading as low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/emarsh/publications/252743)). This is **strong evidence**. The detail of the 1/3/7/14/30-day ladder matters less than the site might assume. Karpicke and Bauernschmidt found that increasing the *total* spacing between reviews produced about a **200% improvement in long-term retention**. They found no evidence that expanding, equal or shrinking gaps were inherently superior ([Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011 summary](https://hapgood.us/2011/07/14/spaced-retrieval-absolute-spacing-enhances-learning-regardless-of-relative-spacing/)). Karpicke and Roediger found that a slightly *delayed* first review improved long-term memory ([Karpicke & Roediger 2007](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/)). For grammar specifically the evidence is **moderate and mixed**. Longer gaps (7 to 14 days) helped intermediate adults learning English tense and clefts. Shorter gaps sometimes helped beginners ([Kasprowicz, Marsden & Sephton 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141710/14/KASPROWICZ_et_al_2019_The_Modern_Language_Journal.pdf); [Suzuki & Hanzawa 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0272263121000358)). The current ladder is reasonable. Stopping at 30 days is its main weakness if the goal is remembering grammar for months.

**Instant feedback** in Practise is also supported. Corrective feedback on learner output has medium, durable effects: immediate d = 0.64, long-term d = 0.53. It is strongest in one-to-one settings (d = 1.08 vs 0.50 in classrooms), which is exactly what software offers ([Li 2010](https://www.academia.edu/2911659/Li_S_2010_The_effectiveness_of_corrective_feedback_in_SLA_A_meta_analysis_Language_Learning_60_309_365)). A 38-class study found essentially **no difference between immediate and delayed feedback** (estimate 0.002) ([ManyClasses 1, Fyfe et al. 2021](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2021/journal-article-abstracts/manyclasses-1-assessing-the-generalizable-effect-of-immediate-feedback-versus-delayed-feedback-across-many-college-classes/)). The timing is therefore fine. What matters is the *content* of the feedback. A bare "right/wrong" is weaker than feedback that names the rule or prompts a second try. That last point is widely accepted, but the meta-analytic figures could not be confirmed for this report.

The **Notice** step's highlighting is supported, but only modestly. Two meta-analyses put the benefit of highlighted forms at d = 0.22 ([Lee & Huang 2008](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/visual-input-enhancement-and-grammar-learning-a-metaanalytic-review/B9D0C50B09928C20C94548B37B29A042)) and g = 0.54 ([Rassaei & Jabbarpoor 2025](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2025-0118/pdf?lang=de)). Two cautions follow. Highlighting **slightly hurt understanding of the text's meaning** (d = −0.26). **Stacking bold, colour and underline added nothing**. Notice is a useful primer, not instruction in itself. The **Write** step is supported in principle, because producing language pushes learners to discover what they cannot yet say. But a self-check list is weak feedback for forms a learner has not yet mastered (inference).

The **Common mistakes** and **Watch** steps have no direct evidence either way. Showing "wrong vs right" is standard ELT practice and fits the idea of noticing the gap. No study was found that tests whether showing wrong forms helps or harms adult learners, so how to present them is **expert practice, not evidence**: always pair a wrong form with the right one, mark it clearly, and base it on real learner errors. Watch is extra input that the site cannot control. Multimedia research shows that interesting but off-target additions ("seductive details") reduce learning (retention d = −0.30) ([Brom & Hannemann, summarising Rey 2012](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)). A long or loosely related YouTube clip could therefore cost more than it gives (inference).

| Step | Verdict | Strength of support |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Notice | Keep; add a meaning question and "spot the pattern" | Moderate (small effect) |
| 2 Rule | Keep; vary the visual by concept type and thin it by level | Strong (explicit teaching works) |
| 3 Common mistakes | Keep short; use real learner errors | Expert practice only |
| 4 Hear it | Keep; make learners *do* something with the form | Inference from input research |
| 5 Practise | Biggest change needed (see below) | Strong for feedback; weak for current item mix |
| 6 Write | Keep; feedback is the gap | Supported in principle |
| 7 Watch | Lowest priority; vet tightly or make optional | No evidence; some risk |
| 8 Remember | Keep; extend beyond 30 days, mix confusable points | Strong |

## Practise drills one structure, so learners never have to choose

The most fixable weakness is in step 5. All nine items test the lesson's single structure, and most are recognition or fill-in-the-form. A learner in the "present perfect" lesson knows every answer is the present perfect. They can score 9/9 by pattern-matching without ever deciding *when* the present perfect is the right choice over the past simple. Real English demands exactly that decision.

Two bodies of research point the same way. The first is about **interleaving**, meaning mixing practice on different but related structures. Brunmair and Richter's meta-analysis found an overall benefit of **g = 0.42**, strongest when categories are **similar to each other** ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). This is **moderate-to-strong evidence** in general. For grammar, the best study found that mixing two confusable Spanish past tenses gave **no benefit within one session but a "substantially better" result** when practice was spread over two weekly sessions ([Pan et al.](https://discovery-imamu.kwaretech.com/EdsRecord/edsbas,edsbas.988BAC9D)). That boundary suits TenseTrail well. Keep the new lesson's first items focused on one structure. Do the mixing in contrast sets and in the spaced Remember step. The meta-analysis also found that pure word memorisation went the other way (g = −0.39). Mixing therefore helps learners *tell rules apart*, not memorise lists such as irregular verbs.

The second is about **meaningful practice**. Skill-acquisition theory, associated with DeKeyser, holds that grammar knowledge becomes automatic only through practice in which the learner holds form and meaning together ([Taie 2014 overview](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/tpls/vol04/09/30.pdf)). Research on processing instruction found that mechanical drills are the weakest form of practice. Meaning-based input and meaning-based output practice both do better ([MDPI 30-year review of processing instruction, 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/16/2/295)). The same review found that the active ingredient was **"structured input"**. These are tasks where learners can only get the answer by processing the form, not by reading highlighted text. A self-study site can automate this easily. An example: "Did this happen yesterday or every day?" with the time word removed, so only the *-ed* ending tells you. This suits English forms that are easy to ignore because something else in the sentence carries the meaning: third-person *-s*, past *-ed*, passives ("who did what?"), plural *-s* and articles. That match is an **inference from the review's mapping of studied structures**.

One more caution applies to practice difficulty. In an app-based grammar study, practice accuracy predicted learning better than the review schedule did ([Kasprowicz et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141710/14/KASPROWICZ_et_al_2019_The_Modern_Language_Journal.pdf)). "Harder" helps only when learners can mostly succeed. Learners will also *feel* that one-structure practice works better, because it goes more smoothly in the moment, even though mixed and spaced practice wins on later tests ([Karpicke & Roediger 2007](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/)). A line of interface copy explaining why reviews feel harder is worth adding (inference).

## Concept type should change the Rule visual and the practice mix, not the skeleton

Are all grammar points alike? Ellis, a leading second-language researcher, says no. Third-person *-s* is "easy to understand as explicit knowledge but hard to use accurately". Articles are "very frequent in the input" yet "impose considerable learning difficulty" ([Ellis 2006, TESOL Quarterly](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/Readings/Ellis%202006.pdf)). Sorting English grammar into three working groups is a synthesis for this report. It is not a tested classification, but it maps cleanly onto the evidence about what works for each.

| Concept type | TenseTrail examples | What the Rule step should look like | What Practise should emphasise | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Easy rule, hard to use automatically** | 3rd-person *-s*, past *-ed*, questions and *do*, word order, passive form, comparatives | Short, direct rule; the current animated form works well | Structured-input items, then many meaningful items; timed "speed rounds" for automaticity | Explicit teaching (strong); processing instruction (moderate–strong) |
| **Easy form, hard concept** | Present perfect vs past simple, modals, conditionals and *wish*, articles | One core-meaning diagram: a timeline for tense, a "force" picture covering both senses of a modal, a "distance from reality" picture for conditionals | Contrast items mixing the confusable pair; "explain why" prompts | Concept-based studies (small, consistently positive) |
| **Really vocabulary** | Dependent prepositions (*interested in*), phrasal verbs, verb patterns (*enjoy -ing*), set article phrases | Chunk bank in context; group by particle meaning where it helps (*up* = completion) | Recall of whole phrases, spaced repetition; less rule work | Expert opinion (Thornbury) plus spacing evidence |

Evidence for the middle row is **promising but small**. With adult intermediate ESL learners, teaching modals through one core meaning beat teaching them as lists of uses ([Tyler, Mueller & Ho 2010](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/aila.23.03tyl)). The authors criticise textbooks that treat *must* (obligation) and *must* (deduction) "as unrelated homophones". A single diagram linking the spatial and abstract meanings of *in/at/over* improved translation more than correct/incorrect feedback alone. It did not beat a rule plus examples, and the study used one session with teenagers and had no delayed test ([Wong, Zhao & MacWhinney](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/01881cdb-62f2-578c-8998-965e19872750/content)). A three-week study of 45 university students compared concept-based teaching with PPP (present, practise, produce). Concept-based teaching gave lasting gains in understanding (d ≈ 1.4). Its advantage in *accurate use* appeared only at the delayed test (d = 1.07) ([Benhamlaoui & Gánem-Gutiérrez 2022](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/32250/1/A%20Classroom%20Application%20of%20CBLI%20January%202022%20accepted%20030222.pdf)). That approach also asks learners to **explain the concept in their own words**, a step TenseTrail currently lacks. The "distance" explanation for conditionals is widely recommended by teachers, but **no direct study of it was found**.

For the bottom row, the evidence for chunks is real but bounded. Thornbury's review reports that nearly 60% of spoken language is formulaic to some degree. Chunks' role in fluency is "uncontroversial" ([Thornbury 2019, Cambridge Papers in ELT](https://www.cambridge.org/gb/files/3815/6941/9472/CambridgePapersInELT_Chunks_2019_ONLINE.pdf)). The same review warns that the idea that chunks alone lead to grammar is "contested". The Lexical Approach was "only sketchily supported by evidence", so chunks should complement rules, not replace them. This is **expert opinion grounded in corpus data**. Applied to Adelaide, the Hear it dialogues should carry local formulas such as *How are you going?*, *No worries* and *Can I grab a flat white?* That is an inference about relevance, not a research finding.

Respected providers do not vary format by concept. British Council LearnEnglish uses the same pre-test, explanation, post-test sequence at A1–A2, B1–B2 and C1 ([LearnEnglish guide](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/english-levels/improve-your-english-level/how-use-learnenglish-grammar-section)). Murphy's *Grammar in Use* uses the same two-page spread for every unit ([Goodreads description](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/823097)). That shows a consistent frame is commercially normal and easy to navigate. It is not evidence that one format is best. TenseTrail's frame is fine. Varying the inside of the Rule and Practise steps is where the gain lies.

## Level should thin the scaffolding; the learner's own knowledge should decide what they skip

The strongest case for variation is by level, and it comes from learning science rather than language research. Kalyuga defines the **expertise reversal effect** as "the reversal in the relative effectiveness of instructional methods as levels of learner knowledge in a domain change". Guidance that helps novices burdens experts, who must check it against what they already know. Novice-versus-expert differences ranged from **0.45 to 2.99** across studies ([Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)). Kalyuga recommends heavy worked examples for novices, a mix for intermediates and minimal guidance for advanced learners. In his studies, **learner-adapted tutorials beat fixed ones** (effect sizes 0.46 to 0.69). The effect is **strong evidence in general**. It has **not been tested on grammar websites across CEFR levels**, so applying it to TenseTrail is inference. It is a well-grounded one. A language-adjacent example points the same way: glosses placed right beside words helped low-level ESL readers but hindered university students.

Language experts add their opinions on top. Ellis suggests that "simple rules may best be taught deductively, while more complex rules may best be taught inductively". Deductively means the rule is given first. Inductively means learners work it out from examples. He adds that learners with stronger analytic skills "fare better with an inductive approach". He also says "grammar is best taught to learners who have already acquired some ability to use the language", since beginners lean on memorised phrases ([Ellis 2006](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/Readings/Ellis%202006.pdf)). He presents these as proposals; the studies he reviews are mixed. Researchers such as Long criticise any fixed "present, then practise" sequence as a default, because learners acquire grammar in their own developmental order ([Cambridge Element: Focus on Form](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/abs/focus-on-form/A998E7C66AB0448C974D67E338772F11)). This is **expert and theoretical critique, not meta-analytic evidence**. A self-study site without a conversation partner cannot run a fully task-based course anyway. The practical takeaway is narrower. Let higher-level learners *try first*, and do not assume nine controlled items produce spontaneous use.

In practice, A1–A2 lessons should open with a useful phrase in context. They should give a very short rule with a step-by-step animation, start with recognition items before typing, and keep writing to sentence frames. B1–B2 lessons can use a semi-authentic text and a contrast diagram. C1 lessons should open with an authentic text (an ABC radio transcript, a workplace email). Learners spot the rule themselves and then check it against a collapsible summary. Practice there should focus on choice, register and nuance, with fewer, harder production items. This level breakdown is **inference from expertise reversal plus Ellis's opinion**. Revisiting the same topic at rising levels, for example modals for permission at A2, obligation at B1, deduction at B2 and past speculation at C1, follows Cambridge's research on which features mark each level ([Hawkins & Filipović 2012](https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV040930895)). The exact level placements from the English Grammar Profile could not be verified for this report.

For individual learners, the rule is simple: **adapt to what they know, not to their "style"**. Pashler and colleagues found "virtually no evidence" that matching teaching to learning styles helps. They concluded there is "no adequate evidence base" for learning-styles assessments ([Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)). This is **strong evidence**. Offer the animation, text and audio to everyone and never route learners by a style quiz. Adapting to demonstrated knowledge is supported. Short diagnostics can predict full test results closely (r up to .92) in a fraction of the time ([Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)). LearnEnglish already opens each lesson with a pre-test ([LearnEnglish](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/english-levels/improve-your-english-level/how-use-learnenglish-grammar-section)). Duolingo shows grammar tips after a mistake ([Duolingo blog 2020](https://blog.duolingo.com/language-rules-learning-grammar-on-duolingo/)). Erlam found that deductive teaching with output practice "may neutralize" differences in language aptitude ([Erlam 2005](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/23883?show=full)). The current rule-first default is therefore a fair choice for most learners, with discovery offered as an option rather than imposed.

Two things remain genuinely unknown. No study was found on whether learners get bored by an identical format across 100+ lessons. Gamification research shows small positive motivational effects (g = .36), and novelty effects confound it ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). Duolingo raised daily engagement 12% with a smarter review schedule ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)). Neither speaks to template fatigue directly. TenseTrail can answer the question for itself by tracking which steps people skip.

## Leads the research notes could not confirm

Several widely repeated claims surfaced during the research but could not be checked against a source. Treat them as things to verify before quoting them on the site or in marketing. The first is the precise effect sizes often attached to the explicit-instruction meta-analyses. One is that Norris and Ortega found explicit d ≈ 1.13 vs implicit d ≈ 0.54. Another is the reported finding that Kang, Sok and Han (2019) saw the explicit advantage **narrow on delayed tests**. If true, that would strengthen the case for spaced review. Unconfirmed claims from well-known books include Larsen-Freeman's view that "use" (when and why to choose a form) is the hardest and most neglected dimension of grammar. They also include Thornbury's and Scrivener's recommendations to vary the sequence and to use test-teach-test at higher levels, Willis's task-first framework, and Swain's "pushed output". All are plausible and fit the evidence above, but none was confirmed. Nakata and Suzuki's (2019) study reporting that mixing grammar exercises improved long-term retention is cited by others but was not read. Lyster's finding that prompting learners to self-correct beats simply showing the right answer was not confirmed either. Finally, the claim that showing learners wrong forms risks them memorising errors has **no evidence either way**.

## What to do, in order

The table below is written for the site owner. Priority 1 items give the biggest learning gain for modest effort. Later items build on them.

| Priority | Action | Why (and how sure we are) | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Change the Practise mix.** Of the 9 items, make roughly 3 controlled (recognition or form), 3 or 4 meaningful (the answer depends on understanding context, with no giveaway time words), and 2 or 3 where learners choose between the new structure and its look-alike (e.g. present perfect vs past simple). End with 1 open "say something true about you" item with a model answer. | Mixing confusable points (moderate–strong) and meaningful practice (moderate–strong) | Content work, no new tech |
| **1** | **Make feedback explain, not just mark.** On a wrong answer, give a second try with a hint, then show the answer plus a one-line rule linked to the Common mistakes step. | Feedback works (strong); explaining is better (widely accepted, figures not verified) | Low |
| **2** | **Add a 4–6 item pre-check to each lesson.** A high score lets learners jump to the harder Practise, Write and Remember steps. A low score takes them through the full lesson. | Adapting to prior knowledge (strong in general); used by British Council | Medium |
| **2** | **Upgrade Remember.** Add reviews at about 60 and 120 days. Shorten the gap after a miss and lengthen it after success. Deliberately pair confusable structures in mixed reviews. Use production items, not repeated multiple choice. | Spacing and retrieval (strong); mixing works best when spaced (moderate) | Medium |
| **3** | **Make the Rule visual fit the concept type.** Timelines for tenses, one core-meaning picture per modal, a "distance" picture for conditionals and *wish*, chunk banks for prepositions and phrasal verbs. Add an "explain it in your own words" prompt for concept-heavy points. | Concept-based teaching (small studies, consistently positive); chunks (expert opinion) | Medium–high (design) |
| **3** | **Thin guidance by level.** A1–A2: start from a useful phrase, short rule, step-by-step animation. B2–C1: start with an authentic Australian text, let learners spot the pattern, and make the rule summary collapsible. | Expertise reversal (strong in general, untested on grammar sites); Ellis (opinion) | Medium |
| **4** | **Sharpen Notice and Hear it.** Use one highlight style only. Add a meaning question to Notice and a "what do the highlighted words have in common?" prompt. Turn Hear it into a task, e.g. "reconstruct the dialogue" or "who did what?". Use Australian voices and phrases. | Highlighting is weak alone (strong); structured input is the active ingredient (moderate–strong) | Low–medium |
| **4** | **Strengthen Write feedback.** Add automated feedback on the learner's own text where feasible. Keep the checklist and model answer as a fallback. | One-to-one feedback has the largest effects (strong); self-checking is weak (inference) | Medium–high |
| **5** | **Demote Watch.** Vet each video for length and exact fit, or label it optional. | Off-target extras reduce learning (moderate); no evidence for the step | Low |
| **Ongoing** | **Measure.** Track step completion and skip rates by lesson and level. Include some delayed, free-production checks, not only quiz scores. | Fills the gap no study answers: whether a fixed format causes fatigue | Low |

Keep unchanged: the eight-step frame and its order for A1–B1, rule-first as the default, instant feedback, the Common mistakes step (short, paired, based on real errors), and spaced review. Do not add: a learning-styles quiz, extra highlighting effects, or decorative animation.

## Conclusion

The question "one format or many?" turns out to be the wrong cut. The research supports a fixed *frame* and a variable *fill*. The eight steps can stay as TenseTrail's recognisable structure, much as British Council and Cambridge keep theirs. What should flex sits inside those steps: the kind of explanation a concept needs, how much help a level needs, and whether a learner needs the lesson at all. The most important shift is in what counts as practice. Right now a learner can finish a lesson without ever choosing between two structures or saying anything of their own. Fixing that costs content time, not engineering. The evidence for it is stronger than for any redesign of the lesson's look.

The honest limit is that none of this has been tested on a self-study grammar website like TenseTrail. The strong findings come from classrooms, labs and general learning science, and the bridge to the site is reasoning. That makes TenseTrail's own data unusually valuable. Skip rates, pre-check scores and delayed production checks would let the site confirm or overturn these recommendations for its own Adelaide learners. Few providers have run that test openly.
